The manual, in nine screens.

Roadbook has three jobs: get you somewhere, follow a roadbook at speed, and make roadbooks. Everything lives behind one big button at the bottom of the screen — sized for a glove — which opens a sheet of giant buttons.

1Install and get maps

  1. On the phone, open roadbook.apk, allow installs from that app, Install.
  2. Open Roadbook, allow Precise location. Allow notifications if you want the recording indicator while the screen is off.
  3. Tap the big ≡ button → MAPS → DOWNLOAD → pick a region, tick Roads and Terrain, DOWNLOAD. Half a gigabyte for a big rural state. Downloads resume if interrupted.

That is the last time the app needs a signal. Packs overlap and route across state lines.

2Go somewhere

  1. Long-press the map → ROUTE HERE. Or ≡ → GO TO and type an address, street or town.
  2. Every mode you've ticked under MODES is drawn at once: Fast, Towing, Backroads, Dirt, Ride, Quiet, Gravel. Tap a line or a row to choose it.
  3. Long-press elsewhere → VIA to pull all routes through a point. FROM plans from somewhere other than where you are.
  4. GO. Or SAVE to keep it as a roadbook (the turns go in as cues) — see step 7.

3The screen while you ride

Heading-up, you in the lower third, the next cue on top. Tap the map to cycle whole route → next turn → close up; pinch to adjust, drag to look around (the crosshair resumes following).

4Sound

  • ≡ → SOUND while navigating. Off by default; remembered.
  • About fifteen seconds out the cue is spoken: "Left. Onto Dry Creek Road." Then short pips rise one octave and quicken as the bar fills. A two-note chime at the turn.
  • A note is read in your words. A danger is announced, then a low buzz at the spot. A danger at the same place as a turn is one instruction: "Left. Danger. Washout." Nothing is skipped.
  • The voice is built into the app. Pair a helmet headset or stereo; the cues go out as navigation audio.
  • Screen off, sound on. On a bicycle press the power button and ride by ear: the voice, the pips and the chimes carry on with the screen dark, and so does a recording. A quiet notification shows the next cue and the distance to go, with an End navigation button; the GPS stays at one fix a second. Expect a few percent of battery an hour instead of the screen's twenty.

5Off the line

  • Leave the route (gas, a look at something) and nothing changes: the banner shows the direction and distance back; one low buzz if sound is on, a short note when you're back.
  • Nothing recomputes by itself. For a planned route, REROUTE asks the router from where you are. A roadbook can't be rerouted — ride back to the line.
  • No fix: the card says WAITING FOR GPS; nothing from the GPS for 15 s: NO GPS · 1:20 and no pips. Nothing is guessed. Poor accuracy shows a ? after the distance.
  • ≡ → END stops. Tap the bottom card for the written turn list and SAVE / SHARE.

6Record a pre-run

  1. ≡ → RECORD. A strip appears with big CUE and STOP buttons. Recording continues with the screen off.
  2. At every turn and hazard, stop and tap CUE: pick the arrow, or NOTE / DANGER (severity 1–3), type what to say. One tap on an arrow is a complete cue; words are optional except for a note.
  3. STOP → SAVE (into your folder) or SHARE. One GPX: the line plus the cues.

A crash or dead battery loses at most a minute; the app offers the unfinished recording next time it opens.

7Edit cues on any track

  1. ≡ → TRACK → pick a GPX → EDIT CUES. (FOLLOW rides it straight away.)
  2. Long-press on the line to add a cue there. Tap a cue to change or delete it. LIST shows the roadbook as text.
  3. SAVE writes over the file, or SAVE AS. FOLLOW starts riding it.
  4. While following, ≡ → ADD CUE HERE adds a cue at your position — for the stop-and-note pass on a route you planned at the table.

8The file

A roadbook is an ordinary GPX. Cues are waypoints whose <type> names them; <desc> carries the words. Any GPX editor can write them, and any map app still opens the file.

<wpt lat="46.0201" lon="-112.0000"><desc>onto the ranch road</desc><type>cue:LEFT</type></wpt>
<wpt lat="46.0302" lon="-112.0001"><desc>washout</desc><type>danger:3</type></wpt>
<wpt lat="46.0403" lon="-112.0000"><desc>water crossing</desc><type>note</type></wpt>

Turns: STRAIGHT, SLIGHT_LEFT, SLIGHT_RIGHT, LEFT, RIGHT, SHARP_LEFT, SHARP_RIGHT, KEEP_LEFT, KEEP_RIGHT, UTURN, ROUNDABOUT. Cues more than 150 m from the line are ignored. A track with cues is followed exactly as written — no turns are invented from its shape.

9The planner, on a big screen

hondoroadbook.com/plan is the same router and the same maps, on a desk. Nothing you do there is stored unless you publish.

  1. Click the map twice: start and finish. Pick the mode above the stats; every mode treats trails as fair game once you drag the line onto one.
  2. Drag the line onto any road or two-track to add a stop and pull the route through it. Drag a stop to move it, click it to delete it. Stops can be added anywhere along the way; the order follows the line.
  3. Click the line for the menu: Add a cue (arrow, note or danger, with words), Add a stop, or Inspect — the research desk for that spot: aerial imagery, what the map knows about the road, the nearest fuel, food, supplies, camping (free ones marked), water, beds and repair from the map's place index with distance and direction — and dropped onto the main map at any zoom while the sheet is open, so you can see where they sit — then the web, section by section in the same order (food, fuel, camping and beds, free camping, supplies, trip reports and forums, trail and road guides) — each searched when you press its button, or all at once, with a link beside it that runs the same search in your own browser. Each section searches for the named places the map found rather than for a coordinate, and a result that names one is marked via that place; a box at the top searches the area for anything you type (hot springs, bike shop). Sections are confined to a list of sites you can edit under sources; an empty list means the open web. Every finding has + note, which puts it on the route as a cue at that spot, link included, so the research rides with you.
  4. The cues come from the router; edit any, delete any, add your own. reset to turns starts over from the router's turns.
  5. Layers (top right): Terrain, Land ownership (BLM surface management: public vs private, with a legend), Aerial, Places (fuel, food, camping, water, beds, repair from the map — click one for + stop, which routes through it and leaves a note there, or + note on the route pointing to it), published Roadbooks, Night. The elevation profile and surface bar sit under the distance. The big climbs become cues on their own: one at the base with the length, gain, average and steepest grade, one at the summit with the height and how far the descent runs — so on the phone "next cue in …" reads as distance to the top. Bike modes get every climb from about 200 ft of gain; motor modes only the passes. Delete any you do not want like any other cue. Roads OpenStreetMap marks private (an access=private tag, or a locked gate) are routed around when anything else connects; when a route has no choice, that stretch is drawn red-dashed, counted as Posted private in the surface bar, and Inspect says so — a heads-up to ask, not a ruling on who may pass.
  6. Send to phone shows a six-letter code. On the phone: ≡ → TRACK → FETCH CODE FROM THE PLANNER. The roadbook lands in your folder and opens. Codes last 24 hours. Download GPX is the same file by hand.
  7. Publish puts it in the shared library: anyone can find it on the planner map or on the phone under MAPS → BROWSE ROADBOOKS, tap it, FOLLOW or SAVE. From the phone, any opened track has PUBLISH TO THE LIBRARY, with a choice to trim the ends so a ride never starts at your door. Published files keep only the line and the cues.

The link in the address bar carries your points and mode, so a plan can be shared as a link before it is ever saved.
