The manual, in nine screens.

Roadbook has three jobs: get you somewhere, follow a roadbook at speed, and make roadbooks. Everything lives behind one big button at the bottom of the screen — sized for a glove — which opens a sheet of giant buttons.

1Install and get maps

  1. Android: on the phone, open roadbook.apk, allow installs from that app, Install. For the newest changes before they are called stable, roadbook-beta.apk installs over it the same way. iPhone: it comes through Apple's TestFlight while in beta — how, in two minutes.
  2. Open Roadbook, allow Precise location. Allow notifications if you want the next cue shown while the screen is off.
  3. Something wrong? MAPS → SEND FEEDBACK starts an email to us with the app version and your phone filled in, and the last crash if there was one — you see it all before you send it. Or write to wiley@machinenusa.com.
  4. Tap the big ≡ button → MAPS → DOWNLOAD → pick a region, tick Roads, Terrain and Place names, DOWNLOAD. Half a gigabyte for a big rural state; place names are a few megabytes, and can be added later to a region you already have without downloading its map again. Downloads resume if interrupted.
  5. Places: zoom in a little and the pack's fuel, camping, water, beds, repair and food appear on the map (the important ones first, a cafe only close up). Tap one: it becomes a pin with its name and hours, ready for ROUTE HERE or VIA. MAPS → PLACES turns them off. Packs downloaded before this was added need a fresh download to carry the places.

That is the last time the app needs a signal. Packs overlap and route across state lines.

2Go somewhere

  1. Long-press the map → ROUTE HERE. Or ≡ → GO TO and type an address, street or town — or, with the region's place names, a pass, spring, peak, canyon, mine or trailhead (Hunter Mountain, Saline Valley Hot Springs). US place names come from GNIS, the federal list, with the everyday names from OpenStreetMap; elsewhere, OpenStreetMap.
  2. Every mode you've ticked under MODES is drawn at once: Fast, Towing, Backroads, Dirt, Ride, Quiet, Gravel. Tap a line or a row to choose it.
  3. Long-press elsewhere → VIA to pull all routes through a point. FROM plans from somewhere other than where you are.
  4. GO. Or SAVE to keep it as a roadbook (the turns go in as cues) — see step 7.

Plan a whole ride on the phone

≡ → PLAN asks where the ride starts (search, or FROM WHERE I AM) and where it ends (or BACK TO THE START for a loop). Then, on the compare screen:

  • STOPS lists start, stops and end by name: move them up or down, drop one, ADD STOP by search, LOOP (the end becomes the last stop and you finish where you started), REVERSE.
  • Long-press the chosen line for a note or a danger at that spot, with the words to say. Tap one of the small rings on it — the router's turns — to say that turn in your own words ("left at the red barn"). Tap any cue to change or delete it.
  • SAVE keeps it all, names included, as a roadbook file; TRACK → open it → EDIT ROUTE brings the whole plan back. No signal needed for any of it.

3The screen while you ride

Heading-up, you in the lower third, the next cue on top; the stretch of route between you and that cue glows in the route's colour. The card at the bottom wraps the big button: what is left and the ETA on its left, your speed and elevation on its right, and a scale bar along its top edge that is always one mile (one kilometre), only its length changing as you zoom. Tap the map to cycle whole route → next turn → close up; pinch to adjust, drag to look around (the crosshair resumes following). The marker points where the GPS says you point — miss a turn and it carries straight on, never round a corner you did not take.

4Sound

  • ≡ → SOUND while navigating. Off by default; remembered.
  • About fifteen seconds out the cue is spoken: "Left. Onto Dry Creek Road." Then short pips rise one octave and quicken as the bar fills. The two-note chime is the moment to turn in — a couple of seconds of road before the junction at your speed, so the bars go when it sounds, not after. Stopped in the approach (a sign, a gap in traffic) the pips fall silent after a few seconds and come back as you move.
  • A note is read in your words. A danger is announced, then a low buzz at the spot. A danger at the same place as a turn is one instruction: "Left. Danger. Washout." Nothing is skipped.
  • The voice is built into the app and runs on the phone, no signal needed: a natural voice that reads each cue as soon as it is next, so it is ready when the time comes (if one is not, the phone's plain voice says it rather than be late). Pair a helmet headset or stereo; the cues go out as navigation audio.
  • Cues are read the way people say them: US-287 as "U S two eighty-seven", CR 14B as "county road fourteen B", a roll chart's R ON RANCH RD !! as "right on ranch road".
  • Screen off, sound on. On a bicycle press the power button and ride by ear: the voice, the pips and the chimes carry on with the screen dark, and so does a recording. A quiet notification shows the next cue and the distance to go, with an End navigation button; the GPS stays at one fix a second. If the phone kills the app mid-ride, the next launch offers to resume the ride where it was — cues already passed are not repeated. Expect a few percent of battery an hour instead of the screen's twenty.
  • Battery saver (≡ → BATTERY SAVER, or on its own under 15 %): one button that does everything that helps. The screen wakes by itself — over the lock screen, no unlocking — when a cue's window opens or the line is lost, and goes dark again after the turn, showing only the next cue in grey; a tap shows the map for twenty seconds. Between cues the GPS eases to one fix every few seconds (never fewer than six fixes before a turn at a speed you will not exceed, and one a second inside 300 m) and the processor sleeps between fixes. Roughly doubles the hours per charge; with airplane mode on in the backcountry (Bluetooth can stay on for the headset — maps and voice need no signal) more than that.

5Off the line

  • Leave the route (gas, a look at something) and nothing changes: the banner shows the direction and distance back; one low buzz if sound is on, a short note when you're back.
  • Nothing recomputes by itself. For a planned route, REROUTE asks the router from where you are. A roadbook can't be rerouted — ride back to the line.
  • No fix: the card says WAITING FOR GPS; nothing from the GPS for 15 s: NO GPS · 1:20 and no pips. Nothing is guessed. Poor accuracy shows a ? after the distance.
  • ≡ → END stops. Tap the bottom card for the written turn list and SAVE / SHARE.

6Record a pre-run

  1. ≡ → RECORD. A strip appears with big CUE and STOP buttons. Recording continues with the screen off.
  2. At every turn and hazard, stop and tap CUE: pick the arrow, or NOTE / DANGER (severity 1–3), type what to say. One tap on an arrow is a complete cue; words are optional except for a note.
  3. STOP → SAVE (into your folder) or SHARE. One GPX: the line plus the cues.

A crash or dead battery loses at most a minute; the app offers the unfinished recording next time it opens.

7Edit cues on any track

  1. ≡ → TRACK → pick a GPX → EDIT CUES. (FOLLOW rides it straight away.)
  2. Long-press on the line to add a cue there. Tap a cue to change or delete it. LIST shows the roadbook as text.
  3. SAVE writes over the file, or SAVE AS. FOLLOW starts riding it.
  4. While following, ≡ → ADD CUE HERE adds a cue at your position — for the stop-and-note pass on a route you planned at the table.
  5. Reading a roll chart (beta): any track with cues can be read as a roll chart, and one sent from the planner carries the planner's tulips, symbols and chart settings. Open it and tap FOLLOW AS ROLL CHART, or while following it ≡ → ROLL CHART: the chart goes on screen in place of the map: the same rows the planner prints, big enough for the bars, with the trip meter above them (beside them, held sideways). The list moves itself as you pass each row, and the next row comes up a little before you reach it. − and + nudge the meter by a hundredth; hold the meter to set it to the row at the top (you are standing at that landmark); swipe to look ahead, and it settles back a few seconds later. ODO: ROUTE reads your distance along the track, so it never drifts; ODO: GPS counts the distance you actually ride, like the bike's trip meter, and ignores a standstill. MAP brings the map back. A small map in the corner shows the line around you, the way ahead up, with a dot for each row; tap it to fold it away. A handlebar remote that pairs as a Bluetooth keyboard works the chart: up and down look a row back or ahead, left and right (or − and +) nudge the meter, the centre button (Enter) sets it to the row at the top. The volume buttons stay the phone's. A chart reaches the phone with Send to phone or Download GPX from the planner, and stays with a ride you publish.
  6. Phone to phone, offline (beta): SHARE BY CODE (on an opened file, or SHARE… on a planned route or a recording) turns the screen into a QR code that changes a few times a second. On the other phone: ≡ → TRACK → SCAN A CODE FROM ANOTHER PHONE, and point the camera at it. No signal, no pairing, Android and iPhone either way. It carries everything Roadbook reads from the file: the line, the planned stops and mode, every cue, and the roll chart. A planned route takes a few seconds; a long recorded day is thinned to within a metre of its line first, so it takes seconds too. Missed frames don't matter: keep the camera on it and it fills in. The camera is asked for the first time you scan, and used for nothing else.

8The file

A roadbook is an ordinary GPX. Cues are waypoints whose <type> names them; <desc> carries the words. Any GPX editor can write them, and any map app still opens the file.

<wpt lat="46.0201" lon="-112.0000"><desc>onto the ranch road</desc><type>cue:LEFT</type></wpt>
<wpt lat="46.0302" lon="-112.0001"><desc>washout</desc><type>danger:3</type></wpt>
<wpt lat="46.0403" lon="-112.0000"><desc>water crossing</desc><type>note</type></wpt>

Turns: STRAIGHT, SLIGHT_LEFT, SLIGHT_RIGHT, LEFT, RIGHT, SHARP_LEFT, SHARP_RIGHT, KEEP_LEFT, KEEP_RIGHT, UTURN, ROUNDABOUT. Cues more than 150 m from the line are ignored. A track with cues is followed exactly as written — no turns are invented from its shape.

9The planner, on a big screen

hondoroadbook.com/plan is the same router and the same maps, on a desk. Nothing you do there is stored unless you publish; a track or chart you are working on is kept in your own browser so a reload does not lose it.

  1. The top of the panel stays put: the roadbook's name (click it to rename) and the tools for the whole roadbook — New (start over), Open (a GPX), Reverse, Loop, then GPX (download), Phone (send to phone) and Publish. The ones that need a route are greyed until there is one. Under the stats, a switch shows the route as Cues or as a Roll chart.
  2. Click the map twice: start and finish. Pick the mode above the stats; every mode treats trails as fair game once you drag the line onto one.
  3. Drag the line onto any road or two-track to add a stop and pull the route through it. Drag a stop to move it, click it to delete it. For a loop, drop END onto START or press Loop: the finish becomes the start (an out-and-back until you drag the line into a circuit), and the app knows not to call that arrival until you have been round. Stops can be added anywhere along the way; the order follows the line.
  4. Click the line for the menu: Add a cue (arrow, note or danger, with words), Add a stop, or Inspect — the research desk for that spot: aerial imagery, what the map knows about the road, the nearest fuel, food, supplies, camping (free ones marked), water, beds and repair from the map's place index with distance and direction — and dropped onto the main map at any zoom while the sheet is open, so you can see where they sit — then the web, section by section in the same order (food, fuel, camping and beds, free camping, supplies, trip reports and forums, trail and road guides) — each searched when you press its button, or all at once, with a link beside it that runs the same search in your own browser. Each section searches for the named places the map found rather than for a coordinate, and a result that names one is marked via that place; a box at the top searches the area for anything you type (hot springs, bike shop). Sections are confined to a list of sites you can edit under sources; an empty list means the open web. Every finding has + note, which puts it on the route as a cue at that spot, link included, so the research rides with you.
  5. The cues come from the router; edit any, delete any, add your own. reset to turns starts over from the router's turns. Under the Cues / Roll chart switch, Find along the route has three helpers: turns adds the turns in a track's own shape (for a ride you recorded), junctions asks the map for the other roads at each cue's junction (for the tulips), and gates asks OpenStreetMap for gates, locked gates, cattle guards and stiles within a few metres of the line, then offers each as a note with the chart's symbol and a link to the map's record. Untick any you know are gone. OpenStreetMap is only as complete as its mappers: no gates found is not a promise.
  6. Layers (top right): Terrain, Land ownership (BLM surface management: public vs private, with a legend), Aerial, Places (fuel, food, camping, water, beds, repair from the map — click one for + stop, which routes through it and leaves a note there, or + note on the route pointing to it), published Roadbooks, Night. The elevation profile and surface bar sit under the distance. The big climbs become cues on their own: one at the base with the length, gain, average and steepest grade, one at the summit with the height and how far the descent runs — so on the phone "next cue in …" reads as distance to the top. Bike modes get every climb from about 200 ft of gain; motor modes only the passes. Delete any you do not want like any other cue. Roads OpenStreetMap marks private (an access=private tag, or a locked gate) are never chosen while any public way connects, however much longer the public way is; only when nothing public does — or you put a stop on one — is the private stretch used, kept as short as possible, drawn red-dashed, counted as Posted private in the surface bar and named in Inspect. The tag is read per vehicle: a road private to motor vehicles but tagged bicycle=yes is open to a bike mode. A heads-up to ask, not a ruling on who may pass.
  7. Phone (send to phone) offers two ways. Show the code puts a moving QR code on this screen: on the phone, ≡ → TRACK → SCAN A CODE FROM ANOTHER PHONE and point it at the screen. Nothing goes through our server and the phone needs no signal. Get a code gives a six-letter code instead, for when the phone isn't beside you.
  8. The six-letter code: On the phone: ≡ → TRACK → FETCH CODE FROM THE PLANNER. The roadbook lands in your folder and opens. Codes last 24 hours. GPX downloads the same file, to copy by hand.
  9. Open a GPX (Open, or the link on the empty panel) to work on a track you already have. The planner asks whether to keep it as recorded — the line stays exactly as the file has it, with its heights, and you add, edit or delete cues on it — or route it on roads in the mode above, through stops taken from the track every mile or so, with the router's turns added and the file's own cues kept. The line under the distance switches between the two at any time; GPX, Phone and Publish take whichever is on the map. A file with only route points (no track) is routed straight away.
  10. Roll charts (the Roll chart switch above the cue list): print the route as a rally scroll chart. Pick the layout: A, the 2.25 in stage-rally scroll (NASA Rally Sport RallyMoto: miles to hundredths, overall and delta mileage over a tulip and the instruction), or B, the 148 mm rally-raid roadbook (FIA/FIM: kilometres, total | tulip | notes, CAP headings, speed zones, waypoints). The preview beside the map is drawn by the same code as the PDF. Each cue's Roll chart tab holds its tulip — drag the arrow to the way out, click beside the junction to add a road, click a road to change it — plus the printed words (with the usual abbreviations one tap away), a caution of ! to !!!, a speed limit, symbols, a section start or an odometer reset. The other roads at each junction come from the map as suggestions; rows still exactly as generated are tagged auto in the preview, never on paper. For a track you rode, keep it as recorded so the mileage matches the ground, and turns (under Find along the route) finds its turns from its shape. Export PDF makes a vector PDF in your browser: a continuous roll, or sheets of strips to cut and tape (Letter, A4, A5), with a ruler to check it printed at 100%. GPX, Phone and Publish keep every chart field, so a chart can be finished later or read on the phone (≡ → ROLL CHART while following it); a chart in progress is also kept in this browser between visits. This is new: tell us what your holder and printer make of it.
  11. Publish puts it in the shared library: anyone can find it on the planner map (the Roadbooks layer: published routes glow, and zoomed out each one is a glowing dot, or a numbered cluster where many sit together, so a lone track in an empty state still stands out) or on the phone under MAPS → BROWSE ROADBOOKS, tap it, FOLLOW or SAVE. From the phone, any opened track has PUBLISH TO THE LIBRARY, with a choice to trim the ends so a ride never starts at your door. Published files keep only the line and the cues.

The link in the address bar carries your points and mode, so a plan can be shared as a link before it is ever saved.

hondoroadbook.com · Marks (★ on the pin panel, or on any place or search result) are your own saved points: a name and a note, drawn on the map and searchable under GO TO. A copy, "Roadbook marks.gpx", is kept in your folder (on iPhone, Roadbook's folder in Files, so it is in your backup) and replaced on every change; open any GPX of waypoints to add or restore marks, and MAPS has EXPORT, IMPORT MARKS and BURN. MAP in the menu cycles day → night → sun; the sun map is for noon on the bars: a white ground (the glass mirrors the sky, and a bright map out-shines that reflection where a dark one has to compete with it), wide dark roads, big labels and nothing else drawn — no land use, buildings, hillshade or small print — so it stays legible through glare and is less to draw. Toggles in the menu (map, sound, battery saver, terrain, lock) leave it open so you can tap again; the rest close it. With it, turn the screen up and let BATTERY SAVER do the saving. LOCK SCREEN in the menu holds the screen the way your mount sits. Miles/km and TERRAIN ON/OFF under MAPS (terrain is also in the menu once a terrain pack is installed).
